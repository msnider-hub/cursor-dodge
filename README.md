# Cursor Dodge

A single-file browser minigame. Steer a marker with your mouse, hug a drifting violet cube for points, and survive twenty rounds of things trying to stop you.

Two rules make the game: **only the violet cube pays**, and **every round is one event on repeat**. Chasing the payout and dodging the round are the same job, pulling in opposite directions.

## Play

Double-click `index.html` — no server, build step, or dependencies. Works in any current Chrome, Edge, or Firefox. The `sounds/` folder beside it holds the two background tracks and the seven sound effects, and the game runs silent rather than broken if it is missing.

## Controls

| Input | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Mouse move | Steer the blue marker 1:1 |
| **Start** / `Enter` | Begin a game (this **captures your mouse** — see below) |
| `-` / `+` | Sound volume down / up, 5 % a press (works mid-game) |
| `M` | Mute or unmute all sound (same as the HUD **SOUND** button) |
| `Esc` | Release the mouse and end the game (the score still counts) |
| **Play again** / `Enter` | Restart from the game-over screen |
| **Change name** | Return to the start screen to edit your name |
| **clear** | Wipe all saved leaderboard scores |

### Mouse capture

Starting a game asks the browser for a **pointer lock**, so the marker is driven by raw mouse movement rather than by where the system cursor happens to be. The practical difference is at the edges: with the lock, running the mouse off the side of the arena and back brings the marker straight back with it, because there is no system cursor sitting out on the desktop for it to be pinned against. `Esc` hands the mouse back and ends the game.

If the browser refuses the lock, the game falls back to absolute tracking — the marker simply goes wherever the system cursor is, clamped to the arena. Either way the marker sits exactly on the pointer, every frame; nothing is interpolated and nothing is owed. Set `USE_POINTER_LOCK = false` in the source to force the fallback; most of the test tools do, since a headless browser will not grant a lock.

## The prize cube

One violet 34 px cube is **always** in the arena, drifting between waypoints. It is the only thing in the game that pays proximity points or feeds the multiplier — every other cube is purely in the way. It is still a cube, so touching it costs a heart like anything else.

Its payout is drawn around it as a **soft violet glow** rather than a marked-out zone. The brightness follows exactly the same curve the multiplier does, so what you see is the rate — and because the glow dissolves into the backdrop instead of ending at a line, there is no safe distance to memorise. You can tell you are being paid more; you cannot tell when you have hit the maximum without going closer.

- **Base rate** — 10 pts/s just for being alive.
- **Proximity** — up to 60 pts/s more, scaled linearly by how close the marker's rim is to the prize cube's surface, out to 155 px.
- **Multiplier** — a `×N` label floats beside the marker and multiplies *everything*. It grows only inside that 155 px, and the growth is **top-heavy**: `2.00/s × prox^1.9`, where `prox` runs from 0 at the outer limit to 1 at point-blank. Capped at ×12. It shifts blue → amber → red as it climbs, flashes each whole number it crosses, and **pulses gently once it is capped out** — the one piece of feedback left when the number has stopped moving.
- **What that curve means** — point-blank is worth 2.00/s, 30 px off is 1.33/s, 60 px off is 0.79/s, 90 px off is 0.38/s, and 120 px off is 0.12/s. Beyond 155 px it is exactly nothing. Half the band away is a bit over a *quarter* of the rate, not half of it; the last few pixels are still where the game is, but the far half of the band now pays enough to be worth standing in while you wait out whatever is crossing the arena.
- **Letting go** — off the prize, a **0.6 s** grace fuse drains under the label, and then the multiplier burns down at **5.0/s** — a couple of seconds of being chased off it and you are back to ×1.
- Taking a hit resets the multiplier to ×1 immediately and starts the decay with no grace.

So the score is a measure of how long you managed to stay glued to the one cube you must not touch. Peak multiplier is reported on the game-over screen and stored with the score.

### It is not a rectangle with a glow

The one cube you spend the whole game looking at acts like it knows you are there. None of it touches the hitbox — it is still a 34 px square for collision and scoring, and every mood is derived from numbers the game already has — but all of it is readable at a glance:

- **An eye**, which tracks the marker: it leans toward you, up to about a sixth of the cube's width, and blinks now and then at intervals you cannot set your watch by.
- **Breathing** — a gentle 5 % swell, faster the closer you are, so a cube being paid attention to looks livelier than one across the arena.
- **Bracket spin** — the four corner marks turn at 1.6 rad/s idle, up to ~4.2 when you are point-blank, and *slower* the longer it is ignored. The thing you are trying to read for points is also the thing telling you how it feels about your last ten seconds.
- **A lean into its own drift** — up to 0.16 rad of tilt off its velocity, so a fast cube looks like it is going somewhere.
- **A flinch.** Anything that is not the marker passing within **40 px** startles it: it goes pale, hops **150 px/s** directly away, and throws out the waypoint it was heading for. Cosmetic in the sense that nothing about the hit test changes; not cosmetic in the sense that a wall going by really does shove the payout off its line.
- **Sulking** — once it starts complaining that it is **LONELY** (see the neglect watch below), it stops steering purely for its waypoint and leans toward you instead, up to half the way, with its eye turned away and drooping. The cube that wants company drifts toward company, which quietly makes ignoring it easier to stop doing.
- **Flecks** rising off it when the marker is close enough to be earning well — the only mood cue that is also a payout cue.

The prize cube never strands itself. When an event carves out a safe pocket — the eye of a spiral, the inside of a closing box — the event publishes it as a `focus` and the cube heads there, moving at 2.2× speed while it is outside. A `focus.keep` pocket — the closed box, or whatever ground the half has left you — is a hard pen: the cube cannot leave it, so it is always somewhere you can legally be.

## Lives

Three hearts, shown in the HUD. Each touch costs one; when the last one goes, the game ends.

- A hit grants **1.7 s of ghosting** — the marker blinks, passes harmlessly through cubes, and earns no proximity points.
- Ghosting will not expire while a cube still covers the marker, so a wall parked on top of you costs exactly one heart rather than three in a row.
- `Esc` ends the game with hearts to spare; the game-over line says *bailed out* rather than *out of lives*.

### The rescue cube

Lose a heart and, **6 s later**, a gold cube turns up carrying a **countdown from 10**. Stay beside it until the count reaches zero and you get the heart back.

- It drifts like the prize cube, it is steered into the same safe pockets, and it is **a cube like any other** — touching it costs a heart, which is a memorable way to fail a rescue.
- Its reach is **140 px**, and unlike the prize cube that edge is **drawn as a dashed circle**. Hiding the payout line is what makes the violet cube interesting; hiding a rescue line would just be a third way to lose a heart. The cube's own ring fills as the count is banked, and the number brightens while it is actually running.
- It counts down only while you are inside that circle and **not ghosting**. Driven off, the count **creeps back up at 0.5/s** — a dodge costs you progress without wiping it, and parking out of reach banks nothing.
- It **fades in over 0.6 s** before it can bite or be banked, and it spawns clear of the marker, so it is never dropped on top of you.
- One chance per round, and none at all in the hunter's two, which have no round clock for the count to race. The cube **leaves with the round** whether you took it or not, and it is never offered if there is not enough round left to finish the count — so an offer always fits the round when it is made, and the only thing that un-fits it is ignoring it. Still down a heart next round? A new one turns up.
- It pays no points and feeds no multiplier. A heart is the whole prize.

**Two clocks, and the cube tells you which is winning.** The count is one; the round it has to be finished inside is the other. The daylight between them only ever narrows — the round burns a second a second while an ignored count creeps *back up* at 0.5/s — so the cube can say how the race is going without ever having to take it back:

| Daylight left | What the cube does |
| --- | --- |
| Over 2.2 s | Gold, as ever. There is room to get there and hold on. |
| Under **2.2 s** | The cube, its reach ring and its number all turn **amber and flash**. Still winnable — but only if you leave now. |
| None | The offer is **written off**: ash grey, its ♥ hollowed out to a ♡, the count struck through with **NO TIME** over it. It stops being able to bite and fades away where it stands over **1.6 s**. |

Amber for a warning and grey for a reward that has lapsed, which is the only colour in the game that means *nothing here any more*. Ghosting on the way out matters: an ash-grey cube you have given up on must not still cost you the heart it was offering. The point of the cue is that abandoning a rescue is a decision with a visible price — you can watch the offer you ignored stop being one, rather than look up later and wonder where the gold went.

## Rounds

**Twenty rounds.** Each one is a single event type on repeat for **20–30 s**, then a 2.6 s breather — 4.4 s of one before a boss round. The HUD shows `ROUND n/20`, a bar along the top edge of the arena fills as the round runs, and each round opens with a banner naming what is coming. The hunter's two rounds are the exception to the clock: they run until the boss is beaten, and that bar counts its lives instead.

The schedule is part fixed, part drawn:

- **Rounds 1–8** are a tour of the seven ordinary events, in the order of the table below: every one of them once, before anything repeats.
- **Every fifth round is a boss** — 5, 10, 15 and 20 — and the two bosses take turns: the **closing box** owns 5 and 15, **the hunter** owns 10 and 20. A boss round is also what closes each difficulty cycle, and neither boss ever turns up outside those four slots. Both of the hunter's rounds are the **siege** rather than a hunter round — same body, different job — and round 20 is the tighter of the two. See *the siege* below.
- **Rounds 9–20** that are not multiples of five are **drawn at random** from the seven, never the same event twice in a row. A fresh draw every game, so the back half is not a script you can learn.

| Round | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Walls** | A train of walls, never twice from the same side in a row, and **two of them crossing at once** as long as they are travelling on different axes — about **ten or eleven** in a round, arriving back to back. The gap is the only way through; with two slabs up, the only way through is where the two gaps cross. |
| **Four corners** | Four charges meet in the middle, and the round **alternates where they come from**: off the four corners, then off the middles of the four edges, then the corners again. Each set's safe ground is the next set's line of fire, so the round keeps turning you 45° around the arena. Corner charges *plus* the ordinary cube traffic crossing the arena — the only round with ambient cubes in the first cycle. |
| **Rocket barrage** | A full patterned barrage, then another one queued straight behind it. Each barrage names its pattern as it starts. |
| **Spiral** | The calm eye of the spiral is not parked in the middle, but it does not wander either: **each vortex opens somewhere new** and then holds that spot until it is spent, and the next one winds in somewhere else entirely. Deliberately the gentlest of the roster — a thin, **slow** stream (300 px/s) around a wide 72 px eye — because the work is reading where the next eye is and getting the prize cube there, not chasing a safe spot that is already moving. |
| **Closing box** | The box shuts around you, and then the pocket **goes hunting** — picking somewhere new to be about every **0.88 s** and dragging its walls (and the prize cube) with it. A roomy 216 px pocket on a slow (142 px/s) prowl: the round's difficulty is meant to be having nowhere else to be, not the walls outrunning you to the next spot. It is steered rather than teleported, so it arcs into each new heading and eases to a stop — the whole box glides instead of jerking. **A boss round** (5 and 15). |
| **Clockwork** | Twelve cubes stand in for the hours on a 246 px dial, and two hands tick round it: a long one, and a short one keeping time at a third of the pace. Nothing sweeps — a hand **sits on its hour** with the ray it is about to take drawn in front of it, then snaps a twelfth of a turn clockwise in 0.2 s. That dwell is the round: it is exactly how long you have to get off the next ray. Twelve ticks is one revolution and one wave. The dial is a puzzle you read rather than a thing you dodge, and a puzzle alone is a quiet round, so it is also dealt **one small hunter, every time** — the only non-boss round guaranteed something that aims. It used to be dealt busy loose traffic across the face as well, and that was one reading job too many: the dial asks to be looked at, and cubes crossing it while you did made the round a matter of luck rather than of reading. It is the one round with **no ambient traffic at all**, late cycles included. |
| **Mini hunters** | **Two** 58 px hunters, half the size of the boss and built on exactly its code, outlining onto the stage 0.45 s apart and staying for the whole round. Each one fires **a single cube at a time** instead of a volley, and out of step with the other, so shots arrive from two places about every 1.5 s. Their shots track slower than the boss's (235 px/s) and commit the same way. They keep clear of one another, which is the only reason two of them read as two rather than as one thing firing twice. Two and not three: the pair is what makes the point — a crossfire is read by standing off the line between them — and a third body put shots on that ground too, which turned a round to solve into a round to out-run. |
| **The hunter** | A 122 px boss body swells into the arena and **stays for the whole round**, prowling on the same damped steering the box's pocket uses. Every couple of seconds its core lights up and it fires a volley of **three or four** small cubes at you, thrown across the same 0.6 rad of spread however many there are, so a bigger volley is a denser one rather than a wider one. Each shot **tracks you for about 1.5 s** — bending onto the marker at 2.6 rad/s, never snapping to it, at a steady 255 px/s you can out-run — and then **commits**: the heading it has at that moment is locked and it accelerates to 880 px/s. Dodging late is what makes it miss, and where you dodge *to* is where the next one is already bending. The body itself is slow, and touching it is a hit like anything else. One of it is a boss; two small ones are the round above. **A boss round** (10 and 20) — and a boss slot is the only way the hunter is ever dealt, so it always arrives with the **siege** it plays there: see below. |
| **Half the stage** | Half the arena disappears under cover — one of the four half-planes — and then that half **walks round the stage**, a quarter turn at a time, clockwise or anti-clockwise, three turns to a wave. A turn retreats the old side while the new one advances at the same rate, which always leaves exactly one safe rectangle: the corner both of them are walking away from. Always somewhere to be, and always further round the way the cover is turning. |

After the twentieth round the game is *cleared* rather than merely survived — the over screen says so.

### One hunter or two

The boss and the mini hunters are the same thing at two sizes. One unit is a body that outlines, swells in, prowls, charges, fires and folds away; a round decides how many of them there are and how hard each one hits.

| | the boss | a mini | a decoy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How many | 1 | 2 | 1 at a time |
| Body | 122 px | 58 px | 58 px |
| Prowl speed | 84 px/s | 112 px/s | 112 px/s |
| Shots per volley | 3–4 | **1** | **none** |
| Seconds between volleys | 2.2–2.0 s | 2.8–2.05 s | — |
| Shot tracking speed | 281–333 px/s | 235–301 px/s | — |

A decoy is the siege's third kind: a mini with its volley switched off and a shield switched on, and the only body in the game that never shoots at anything — walking into one still costs a heart. A mini is faster on its feet and slower with its shots, which is the trade that keeps a pair of them fair: what makes the round hard is being aimed at from two directions, not the speed of any one cube. Their volley clocks start at random offsets, so they never fire together — with two bodies on a ~2.7 s cycle, something fires about every 1.4 s.

Bodies **keep clear of one another**. Picking a new spot to stand already scores candidates against where the other bodies are, but that says nothing about what happens on the way to one, so a body also feels a shove away from anything standing too close, and — because a shove is a force and a force can be out-run — it slides clear if it ever actually overlaps, along whichever axis is the shorter way out and half a pixel past touching, so two bodies grazing each other cannot come to rest on exactly the contact distance and spend a second arguing about whether they are clear. Two hunters in a heap would read as one hunter with two rates of fire, which is the one thing the round cannot afford.

### The boss rounds

There are two bosses — the **closing box** and **the hunter** — and they alternate: box on 5 and 15, hunter on 10 and 20. Neither is ever something you walk into:

- The breather before one runs **4.4 s** instead of 2.6, and **☠ BOSS ROUND ☠**, with the round number and the name of what is coming, sits in the middle of the empty arena for all of it. The banner above is about the round you just finished — *ROUND 4 CLEAR*, *brace yourself*.
- The round itself opens on a red **BOSS · CLOSING BOX** or **BOSS · THE HUNTER** banner, then runs with a **pulsing red frame** around the arena and a red progress bar instead of a violet one. The HUD reads `ROUND 5/20 ☠`, in red.
- Surviving it says **BOSS DOWN**.

For the box it is framing rather than a difficulty spike — it keeps the roomy pocket and slow prowl it was deliberately eased to. What makes it a boss is that it is the round with nowhere else to be. The hunter is the other kind: a single thing in the arena with you that aims, which no other round does — and it does not want the arena, it wants the cube. Both of its rounds are the siege below, which is why it publishes no safe pocket: for most of the round there is no loose cube to publish one for.
### The siege: both of the hunter's rounds

The hunter is only ever dealt as a boss, and a boss hunter does not play a hunter round — it plays the **siege**. It opens by **swallowing the prize cube**, which is drawn as a pulsing red cell inside the body, wide-eyed and wobbling, and goes wherever the body goes. While the cube is in there the game **stops paying**: the multiplier reads ×0, the proximity band pays nothing however close you stand, and the score on the HUD does not move. There is no round clock either — the bar along the top edge counts the **boss's three lives** instead, filling as it loses them, and the round ends when the third one goes rather than when a timer runs out. No rescue cube is offered, because a rescue is a race against the round clock and this round has not got one.

The way out is the boss's own reinforcements. Every so often it sends out a **decoy**: a mini hunter, outlined in cyan rather than red, that never fires, never charges and has no volley clock at all. What it does have is a **shield** — a cyan ring around it — and one at a time is all it ever sends.

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **Follow it** | Stand within the decoy's band (**150 px**, 135 in round 20). The ring is eaten away as you hold there: a few seconds of it opens the shield. Ghosting does not count, and stepping away lets the ring seal back up — the work has to be done while you can be hit for doing it. |
| **Bait it** | An open shield is drawn as a dashed amber ring. Get the decoy **between you and the boss** and let a volley come: a boss shot that lands on an unshielded decoy takes it out, and the **boss loses a life**. A shot that lands on a shield that is still up is simply **eaten** — a white flash, and a wasted volley. |
| **Three times** | Three lives, three decoys. A banner marks each hit, along with the cue a cleared round gets, and the third one says **CUBE SAVED**: the cube is let out, the scoring comes back on for the few seconds that are left, and the round then closes itself. Surviving it still says **BOSS DOWN**, and after round 20 the game is *cleared* as it always was. |

Nothing else in the arena can touch a hunter, which is why the round is built entirely out of the boss's own volleys, and why a decoy is worth walking towards rather than away from.

**The cube talks you through it.** This is the one round where it stops being sarcastic: it says what has just happened to it, then gives one instruction at a time — wait for a small one, go and stand with it, hold there, now get behind it — always the instruction that is currently any use, repeated on a long timer rather than said once, because a round with no clock and no scoring is a round a player can be stuck in. Those lines outrank everything else it has to say. The moment it is out it goes straight back to being rude about your driving.

**And it sounds different.** The hunter is the only thing in the game with its own music: `sounds/BG_boss_music.mp3` takes over for rounds 10 and 20 and the ordinary track fades out under it, swapping back at the next round. The switch happens on the round rather than in the breather before it, so the banner and the music land together.

**Round 20 is the same fight, tighter.** You meet the siege twice, and the second time asks nothing new of a player who solved the first one — only more of it:

| | round 10 | round 20 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shots per volley | 3 | **4** |
| Seconds between volleys | 2.2 s | **2.0 s** |
| Shot tracking speed | 281 px/s | 333 px/s |
| Seconds of tracking before it commits | 1.6 s | 1.8 s |
| Standing-with-it band | 150 px | **135 px** |
| Seconds of that to open a shield | 4.5 s | **5.5 s** |
| Shield resealing, per second away | 0.30 | **0.40** |

Everything else is the same round: three lives, one decoy at a time, the same 58 px body at the same speed, the same waits either side of it. The two tracking rows are the hunter's ordinary cycle scaling — rounds 10 and 20 are cycles 2 and 4 — and the other five are dealt as a straight pair rather than off that curve, because the siege is only ever met at those two rounds and a curve through two points would be three quarters invention. More shots coming round sooner is a harder round to stand in and an easier one to bait with, which is the trade: what actually makes round 20 longer work is the decoy, held closer, for longer, giving back more of it every second you are not there.

### What a new cycle changes

A cycle is five rounds, so a cycle boundary and a boss round are the same thing (`tier()` = 0–3). There are seven ordinary events against that five, which is why the clock, the mini hunters and the half first turn up in cycle 2 — their base numbers are set knowing they are never seen at tier 0. The boss hunter is stronger still: it only ever plays rounds 10 and 20, so its numbers start at cycle 2 and it is never met at its base at all.

Each cycle turns every screw at once:

| | cycle 1 | cycle 4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prize cube speed | 122 px/s | 180 px/s |
| Wall gap / speed | 104 px, 240 px/s | 77 px, 348 px/s |
| Corner size / stop distance | 70 px, 56 px from centre | 88 px, 35 px |
| Rockets per barrage / speed | 8, 960 px/s | 14, 1170 px/s |
| Spiral cubes / speed | 22, 300 px/s | 40, 408 px/s |
| Box pocket / roam time / new spot every | 216 px, 3.4 s, 0.88 s | 180 px, 6.1 s, 0.61 s |
| Clock dwell / tick | 0.58 s, 0.20 s | 0.40 s, 0.14 s |
| Mini volley gap / shot speed | 2.80 s, 235 px/s | 2.05 s, 301 px/s |
| Half hold / quarter turn | 1.5 s, 0.85 s | 0.90 s, 0.49 s |
| Ambient cubes | the corners round only | every round (from cycle 3) |

The boss hunter is only ever met in cycles 2 and 4, and what it plays there is the siege, so its volley does not come off this table at all — the two sets of numbers it is actually met with are in *the siege* above. What the cycle does give it is the tracking on its shots: 281 px/s for 1.6 s at cycle 2, 333 px/s for 1.8 s at cycle 4.

The body itself never changes: 122 px, 84 px/s of prowl, and a committed shot always leaves at 880 px/s.

The clock also grows a second hazard: its long hand sprouts a stub out the back from cycle 3, so the ray behind a hand stops being safe ground. Its escorting mini is not tiered at all — a clockwork round has one from the first you meet, which is cycle 2 anyway.

The gap between waves inside a round also shrinks by 15 % of the base each cycle — 45 % shorter by the last one — and ambient cubes get faster and start arriving in pairs.

### Event telegraphs

Nothing arrives unannounced.

| Event | Telegraph |
| --- | --- |
| **Rocket barrage** | Every line in the pattern flashes for 1.5 s, with a pip just inside the edge each shot will arrive from. The next line to fire strobes; queued lines stay dim and spent ones fade out. |
| **Wall** | 1 s of red along the edge it comes from, with the gap marked in blue — and in a round of walls that second runs *while the previous slab is still crossing*, so the next side is announced well before that slab is clear. |
| **Four corners** | Amber brackets pulse where the wave is coming from: L-shaped ones in the four corners, or — on an edge wave — a squared gate at the middle of each edge, a line along the edge with a tick turning inwards at either end. Which shape is pulsing is which set of four is about to charge. |
| **Spiral** | Amber arcs sweep around the eye — and the dashed eye circle stays drawn for the whole wave, because it is the one thing worth tracking. Where it is drawn is where it stays, so the next vortex announcing itself somewhere else is the only movement to read. |
| **Closing box** | A dashed square marks the pocket before the walls arrive. |
| **Clockwork** | The dial and its twelve hour marks pulse in amber before any cube lands, and then on every hour the ray each hand will take next is drawn dashed in red, brightening as the tick comes. Its escorting mini outlines in the same dashed red square as any other body, at the top of the round rather than with a wave. |
| **Mini hunters** | The same dashed red square as the boss, one per body, 1.1 s before that body can bite — and because they arrive 0.45 s apart you read the two outlines in sequence rather than both at once. Each single shot is charged, aimed down a muzzle line, and coloured amber-then-red exactly as the boss's are. |
| **The hunter** | A dashed red square marks the body for 1.1 s before it can bite, and it is harmless while it swells into that outline. Each volley is telegraphed twice over: the body's **core lights up** over the 0.6 s before it fires, and a dashed red **muzzle line** points at where it is aiming while it charges. In flight a shot is **amber with a short streak** while it is still tracking you, and turns **red with a long one** the instant it commits — the colour is the warning that dodging it is now just geometry. |
| **Half the stage** | A dashed amber outline of the side about to be covered — then, while a side is held, a dashed red outline of the side it is about to turn into, brightening as the turn comes. The ground still yours is outlined in faint blue throughout. |

The barrage always uses one of three fixed patterns, never random lines, so it is something you can learn to read:

| Pattern | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| **Sweep** | Evenly spaced parallel lanes, all travelling the same way. It fires every *other* lane first and then comes back for the ones it skipped, so the safe lanes you just used are the next to go. |
| **Ladder** | The same interleaved order, but every second shot is reversed, so they arrive from alternating sides. |
| **Star** | Every line runs through the middle, rotating a fixed step each shot. The centre is the one place you cannot be. |

## How it works

- **Rounds** — a round runs waves back to back until its 20–30 s target is up. A new wave only starts if there is room left for it, so the round ends on a wave boundary rather than cutting one off mid-flight; `ROUND_MIN` is a hard floor, so the rounding can never make a round short. How far a round can land from its own target is therefore set by how long one of its waves runs: a wall is four seconds and a clock or a half is ten, so those rounds miss by several seconds either way. The alternative is cutting a wave off in mid-air, which is worse.
- **The schedule** — `buildSchedule()` lays out all twenty rounds up front, at the start of each game: a boss into every fifth slot, taken from `BOSS_ROTA` in turn, the ordinary events in order into the slots left in the first eight, and a draw from those same events for the rest, rerolling anything that would repeat the round before it. The roster is walked with a cursor rather than indexed by round number, so adding a boss shifts nothing: bosses are filtered out of the pool, and the opening tour simply flows around the slot the boss took. Rounds read their event out of that list, so the HUD, the banners and the *next:* line all agree on what is coming without anything having to predict it.
- **The wall train** — a round of walls is *one* wave that keeps going, which is what lets the walls come thick. The moment a slab is a second from leaving the board, the next wall starts flashing its side, so the 1 s telegraph is paid out of the crossing time rather than added to it. The train stops itself when the round has no time left for another crossing, which is what keeps a wall round inside its 20–30 s now that nothing else ends the wave — but never before the round's 20 s floor, because bowing out there only has the round start a *fresh* train behind it, which overruns by just as much and spends a second of dead air paying for the 1 s telegraph twice.
- **Two walls at once** — a slab no longer waits for the board to clear: the next one goes in once the one in front is **45 %** of the way over, which gets **ten or eleven** walls into a round where waiting a whole crossing managed seven. What it does wait for is an axis. Two slabs crossing *perpendicular* to each other always leave somewhere legal — one gap is a band across the arena, the other a band down it, and bands that cross meet in a rectangle — so the next wall is planned onto the other axis from the one in the air, and never released while another slab is travelling the same way. Two head-on slabs would have no such guarantee: their gaps need not line up, and a wall with no gap in it is not a wall, it is a hit. Measured across a round, the tightest that crossing rectangle ever gets is about 100 px, against the 18 px the marker actually needs.
- **Corners, then edges** — the two sets of four are the same charge with a different set of starting spots (`cornerSpots()`), and which one a wave uses is a single flag flipped as each wave is built. It is reset at the top of every round, so a corners round always opens on the corners the name promises and the alternation is read off from there rather than inherited from the last one. The telegraph reads the same flag, so the brackets always describe the wave that is actually coming.
- **The spiral's spacing** — the gap between entries is tied to how fast they travel, so slowing the stream down meant spacing it out in time as well (0.16 s → 0.19 s alongside 370 → 300 px/s). Slowing the cubes alone would have left *more* of them in the air at once, since each takes longer to cross while they keep arriving just as often — the opposite of easier. The cubes, each given more room, is the point. The count itself is `SPIRAL_ARMS + SPIRAL_ARMS_T * tier()` — **22 arms** plus six a cycle, trimmed about 15 % from what it was. A vortex is a fixed number of arms rather than a duration, so taking arms out shortens the wave as well as thinning it, which is the round getting shorter *and* airier and is why a modest trim is felt.
- **The clock's hands** — the arena only knows axis-aligned rectangles, so a hand is a run of 30 px cubes spaced 26 px along its ray: consecutive links overlap even on the diagonal, and there is no hole to thread. Hour cubes and hand links are event-owned — a `dead` flag their own `step` reads — so twelve ticks later the dial and both hands leave together.
- **The clock's escort** — the guaranteed mini is dealt in `beginRound`, not by the event maker, because the dial re-arms several times in a round and one body per wave would be a swarm by the bell. It is the same `makeHunterUnit` the boss round and the neglect watch use, spawned once and living until the round ends, so nothing had to learn about it: it prowls, shoves, folds away and is counted exactly like any other body. The tests tell it apart from the dial by asking the debug hook what kind each rectangle is (event cube, hunter body, hunter shot, traffic) rather than by switching it off.
- **A rescue's two clocks** — `slack = (roundLen - roundT) - left` is the whole cue. It cannot widen: the round's remainder falls a second a second, and the count either falls at the same rate (you are there) or creeps up at `HEART_SLIP` (you are not), so `slack` is monotonically non-increasing and *written off* is a one-way door. That is what lets the states be drawn as a straight sequence — gold, amber under `HEART_TIGHT`, ash grey once it has gone negative, gone `HEART_LEAVE` later — with no path back that would make a player doubt what grey means. The cube is ghosted at the moment it is written off rather than when it finishes fading, so the lapsed offer can never still take the heart it was there to hand back.
- **The prize cube's moods** — every one of them is derived, not stored: `prox` and the neglect count are already computed for scoring and for the watch, `elapsed` drives the breathing and the blink, and the only new state is a single `fright` float that decays at 2.4/s. That matters because the cube is pinned in place by several tests and by most of the shot frames — a mood that needed its own update history would render a blank cube in exactly the frames that are meant to document it. The flinch is the one mood with teeth: it adds a velocity impulse (`ORB_HOP`) away from whatever came inside `ORB_SCARE` and drops the current waypoint, so it is bounded by the same drift the cube always had. Everything else is draw-time transform, which is also why none of it can move the hitbox.
- **Where the next eye opens** — `spiralSpot()` keeps only the last eye and picks the furthest of a handful of candidates from it, stopping early once one is 300 px clear. That is enough to stop two vortices running in the same corner, which would read as one long wave in one place — the thing this round was changed to get away from. The inset is 165 px so the 72 px eye is never half off the board, which leaves the eye 270 px of height to move in against 570 px of width: expect the spread across a round to be wider than it is tall.
- **Half the stage** — the two sides in play during a turn are neighbours on the way round and therefore perpendicular, which is what makes the uncovered ground a single rectangle rather than an L. Cover is exactly half the stage at rest and a little under mid-turn, where the two strips overlap in a corner. That rectangle's inscribed circle is published as the prize cube's `focus.keep`, so the cube can never be stranded behind the cover. A strip with no depth is parked off the board rather than left as a zero-width line along an edge — the marker is clamped to those edges, and a line there would be a hit you cannot back away from.
- **The neglect watch** — on any non-boss round, the game counts the seconds you spend outside the prize cube's paying band. Once that count passes **half the round's elapsed time**, a single mini hunter outlines in and stays until the round is over; the mini-hunter round, which arrives with two of its own, allows a **third** on the same terms. Read literally the half-the-time rule would fire in the opening seconds of every round, since the marker always starts away from the cube, so it carries a floor of **8 s** of accumulated neglect, and it will not spawn with under 4.5 s of round left. Ignoring the payout is supposed to be the safe, boring option; this is what makes it not free. The rule itself is not written down anywhere in the game, but it is not silent either: the cube asks. Once the neglect is about halfway to the trigger, an amber dashed ring closes in on the prize cube with **LONELY** over it, beating faster and tighter the nearer the trigger gets — and the moment a body has actually been sent for, that turns red, flashes, and throws one ping outwards as it fades. Amber for a warning and red for something already on its way, the same colour language as every other telegraph, on the cube rather than in the HUD: it is the cube being ignored, and what it is warning about will arrive wherever you happen to be standing instead.
- **The hunter's shots** — homing that commits. While `t < home` the shot turns toward the marker by at most `HUNT_TURN * dt` a frame, which is why it can be out-run but not out-waited; after that the heading is simply left alone and the speed is eased up to `HUNT_DASH`. The volley's spread is a *total*, divided among however many shots there are (`HUNT_FAN / (shots - 1)` between neighbours), because the fan is only there to stop two cubes leaving stacked on one another. Handing each extra shot its own 0.3 rad instead would have put the outer pair of a four-shot volley far enough off aim to fly past you rather than bend on — a shotgun, which is a different weapon. Committing is *not* a new aim — the shot keeps whatever heading it happened to have, so a late dodge is what makes it miss. Nothing is re-aimed after that point, which makes the last second of a volley readable rather than a chase.
- **One unit, several users** — `makeHunterUnit()` is the whole hunter: outline, swell, prowl, charge, fire, fold away. The siege asks for one large one and a decoy at a time, the mini round for two small ones, and the neglect watch for a single small one mid-round; a decoy is that same unit handed `{ hold: true, shield: 1 }`, which parks its volley clock and gives it a ring. They all live in one `prowlers` list that is stepped and drawn in one place, which is why a mini spawned by the watch behaves exactly like one dealt by the round, and why the separation shove can see every body regardless of who asked for it. A unit is spawned once and lives until the round is over, reusing the wall train's test for that (`roundT < ROUND_MIN || roundT + HUNT_EXIT <= roundLen`), so a round can never start a body on the scraps of time left at the end of it.
- **The siege** — `isSiege(n)` is a *slot* test, not a round-number one: the hunter's event in a multiple-of-five slot plays `makeSiege()`, anything else plays the ordinary `makeHunter()`. Written that way, the two rounds cannot drift apart — there is one maker, and round 20's difference is a single `late` flag picking the second of each pair of numbers. The round with no clock is the clock turned off: `roundLen = 1e9` at the start of it, and `roundT = roundLen` the moment the cube is freed, so the ordinary wave-boundary code ends the round rather than a second path that knows how to. `makeHunter()` is still there and still tested, but nothing in a real game deals it any more — it is reachable only through `FORCE_ROUND`, which is what the `hunter` scenario drives to measure the shot mechanics both rounds share, far more cheaply than a siege can.
- **How a body moves** — the same critically damped spring as the box's pocket, but with a looser edge backstop: the pocket is ground you have to stand on and so is held strictly inside the arena, while a body is a thing to stay away from and is allowed to hang over an edge rather than dump its momentum into one and stop dead. The prowl keeps running through the fold-away at the end of the round, for the same reason. Bodies also push each other apart, and the shove is inverse rather than linear in the gap — gentle where they are merely near each other, and stiff enough close in to beat the spring, which can be pulling a body towards a spot on the far side of another one. A linear shove loses that argument: it settles at whatever distance the two forces cancel, which measured about 56 px between two 58 px squares — overlapping. Hence the hard slide as well, undoing any overlap along the shorter axis out. With the shove doing the real work that is a pixel or two at a time rather than a jump.
- **Events** — each one is a maker function returning `{ update(dt) -> alive, draw?, quiet?, focus? }`. `quiet` silences ambient spawning (the closing box does, because there is nowhere to dodge to); `focus` publishes the safe pocket for the prize cube to ride in.
- **Cubes** — each ambient cube picks a random size (22–56 px), a random lane, and a single axis, crossing at a constant speed until it leaves the far side. **At most 26 ambient cubes exist at a time**, and that cap counts the traffic layer *only* — traffic is tagged as it is spawned and the tally walks that tag, not `cubes.length`. Counting everything meant an event with a lot of bodies of its own silently cancelled the scenery the round asked for: the clockwork dial is 26 cubes on its own, so a round configured for busy traffic got none at all until the dial was spent — which is academic for the clock now that it is dealt no traffic, and still not academic for the late cycles of every other event that carries furniture. Anything can be a rectangle, so wall pieces are trimmed to leave an exact gap and the box walls are slabs drawn with cube seams.
- **The closing box** — the pocket is the hole and the four slabs are exactly its complement, each reaching 900 px outwards. That is what makes the whole box movable by moving one point: `layoutBox` rewrites four rectangles from the pocket's centre and half-width every frame.
- **How the box moves** — the pocket has a velocity, and its target pulls on it through a critically damped spring (`BOX_STEER`), capped at the roam speed. It still picks a spot far from where it is and still gets there in a hurry, but it accelerates into the turn and decelerates onto the mark instead of snapping to a new heading and stopping dead. Critical damping is the point: it never overshoots, so the pocket never has to be bounced off the arena edge. In numbers, the pocket's velocity now changes by under 26 px/s in a frame, where stopping dead from the roam speed would be a 142 px/s change and a reversal 284. The same steering drives the hunter's body.
- **Collision** — circle-vs-rectangle against the marker's 9 px radius. The same distance drives scoring, but only for the prize cube: negative means a hit, 0–155 px is the paying band.
- **No escape hatch** — there is no teleport. `Space` used to hop the marker 150 px, and every hazard was drawn around the assumption that you might; now the only thing that moves the marker is the mouse, so a wall's gap is genuinely the only way through a wall. The key is still swallowed, because left alone it scrolls the arena off-screen and re-fires whatever button last had focus.
- **Draw order** — the prize cube is drawn after everything else, so a wall or a slab can never hide the one cube you are trying to follow.
- **Timer** — real frame deltas, clamped to 1/30 s so a tab switch can't teleport cubes through you.
- **Music** — two `<audio loop>` elements, the ordinary track and the hunter's, started from the Start-button click, since browsers only allow autoplay off a user gesture; a refused `play()` is swallowed rather than thrown. Only ever one of them is audible: starting a track fades the other one down, so the pair is driven as a single switch (`switchTrack`) that `beginRound` throws on the round's own name rather than anywhere that would have to predict it. The fade is per element — a track can be fading out while the other plays at full volume — and the slider, mute and the end-of-game fade all reach both of them, because a player who turns the sound down means all of the sound. Each track restarts from the top when it is switched to, and the game ends with whatever is playing fading over ~0.5 s.
- **Sound effects** — seven cues and no more: a heart lost, the run ending, a hunter arriving, a round cleared, a rocket leaving, a hunter charging, and each cube of the volley that charge was for. The round-cleared cue does double duty in a siege, where it rings for each life taken off the boss — the same small win, and cheaper than an eighth file. A decoy's shield dropping deliberately gets no cue at all: anything charge-like there would read as *incoming*, which is the opposite of what has just happened. Everything else is telegraphed on the canvas and stays that way, since a cue per cube would drown the seven that matter. Each one is a small **ring of `Audio` elements played in turn** rather than a single element rewound — a barrage fires every fifth of a second and a volley leaves every eighth, and rewinding would cut the shot before it short. Rings are sized to how many can actually overlap, measured against each file's own length: four for hunter shots (0.34 s apiece against shots 0.13 s apart), three for rockets (0.39 s against a shot every 0.20–0.37 s), for hunter charges (a second long apiece, and three bodies can be on the stage charging on their own clocks) and for hunters arriving (0.70 s against the 0.45 s stagger), two for hits, one for the two end-of-run cues, which cannot overlap themselves. The hunter's two sit deliberately **below the rocket** in the mix, because they are the most repetitive sounds in the game — a boss volley is up to six cubes and comes round every 1.25 s. Where each one fires is chosen so it never says something the screen contradicts: the **game-over** sting only on a run that actually ran out of hearts — bailing out with `Esc` is a decision, not a defeat, and clearing all twenty gets the round-cleared fanfare instead — the **hunter** cue on the body swelling in rather than the outline, because a mini round's pair outline together and only swell 0.45 s apart, so firing on the outline would stack both copies on one frame and sound like one — and the **charge** cue on the crossing into the 0.6 s tell rather than on the tell being lit, so one volley gets one cue and not one a frame. The charge file runs a second against that 0.6 s tell, so its tail carries on under the shots it announced, which is what a charge going off is meant to sound like.
- **Volume** — the HUD's **SOUND** panel holds a slider, and it starts at a deliberately modest **30 %**. It governs the effects as well as the track — there is one audio control in the game, and a player who turns it off means all of it — with each effect carrying its own level on top of it, since a cue has to be heard over a track sitting that low. Under mouse capture there is no pointer left to drag it with, so `-` and `+` drive the same setter in 5 % steps at any time, and the slider tracks them. Turning it *up* while muted unmutes — turning it down does not. Both settings live in `localStorage`, under `cursor-dodge.vol` and `cursor-dodge.muted`. The end-of-game fade steps down by a tenth of the current volume, so a quiet track fades over the same half-second a loud one does instead of cutting out at once.

## Leaderboard

The top 10 **scores** are kept in `localStorage` under `cursor-dodge.scores.v2` (the name field lives in `cursor-dodge.name`), so they persist between sessions but stay local to that browser profile. Hovering a row shows the time survived, the peak multiplier, and when it was set; your latest entry is highlighted. Boards saved before scoring existed used `cursor-dodge.scores` and are not shown — **clear** deletes both keys.

The `tracking` scenario is the one that earns its keep by measuring nothing interesting: it asserts a *zero* offset between marker and pointer on every frame of a long sweep, which is the property the old teleport kept quietly breaking.

The four test tools expect Edge at its default install path and exit non-zero on failure. They patch the game by exact-string replacement — `USE_POINTER_LOCK`, `MAX_LIVES`, `ROUND_MIN/MAX`, `MAX_ROUNDS`, `ROUND_BREAK`, `BOSS_BREAK`, `FORCE_ROUND` (set it to an event name and the whole schedule is that one event, which is how a scenario watches a series of them), `ambientRate()`, and `startWave`'s maker call (to get an empty arena) — so if you rename or reformat those lines, the scripts' `assert` guards will tell you. A couple of shots also pin the coin-flips inside an event — which side a wall comes from, which way the half turns — so the same frame comes out twice; pinning the wall side also puts the train back in single file, since two slabs never share an axis, which is what makes it useful for the frames where the walls are only the backdrop, the neglect shots park the prize cube in one corner with the marker in the other so the watch fires on cue, and the two boss-volley shots pin the volley to the four cubes a player actually meets, since every shot is pinned to round 1 and the boss never plays one. `HEART_DELAY` and `HEART_COUNT` are patched too, to put the rescue on a stopwatch a test can watch — and, in tests that count hearts for other reasons, to push the delay out of reach so a life is never handed back under them. The two frames of a rescue losing its race pin `ROUND_MIN/MAX` to a known 22 s and `HEART_COUNT` with them, since a count the game is free to creep back up to 10 cannot be staged at 30 by a debug hook: `runHeart` clamps it to `HEART_COUNT` on the very next frame. `IDLE_MIN` gets the same treatment, and more widely: almost every scenario parks the marker in the middle of the arena and lets the prize cube wander, which is exactly the behaviour the neglect watch answers, so a scenario counting the cubes of one event pushes the watch out of reach unless the watch is the thing it is measuring. The smoketest leaves it live, since a whole game is where an extra body ought to be able to turn up. Tunable numbers the tests care about (`MAX_LIVES`, `BOSS_EVERY`, `WALL_WARN`, `WALL_SPEED`, `SPIRAL_SPEED`, `SPIRAL_EYE_R`, `SPIRAL_INSET`, `CLOCK_R`, `CLOCK_TICKS`, `HALF_TURNS`, `HEART_DELAY`, `HEART_COUNT`, `HEART_BAND`, `MULT_GAIN`, `MULT_CURVE`, `HUNT_SIZE`, `HUNT_TRACK`, `HUNT_TURN`, `HUNT_HOME`, `HUNT_DASH`, `HUNT_VOLLEY`, `MINI_SIZE`, `MINI_COUNT`, `MINI_MAX`, `MINI_VOLLEY`, `MINI_TRACK`, `MINI_STAGGER`, `IDLE_MIN`, `IDLE_PART`, `IDLE_LEFT`, `LONELY_AT`, `LONELY_FLASH`, `HEART_TIGHT`, `HEART_LEAVE`, `SPIRAL_ARMS`, `SPIRAL_ARMS_T`, `ORB_SCARE`, `ORB_HOP`, `ORB_CLING`, `WALL_FOLLOW`, `CORNER_SIZE`, `CORNER_WARN`, `MUSIC_VOL`, `VOL_STEP`, …), along with the round roster, the boss rota and each event's wave length, are read out of the running game through a debug hook instead, so changing one re-aims the assertions rather than breaking them. The exported PNGs have a transparent background (the dark backdrop is CSS, not canvas), so they look white-backed in an image viewer.
